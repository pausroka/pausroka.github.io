Case study
Auronia · 2019–now

When product photography stopped scaling

Over seven years, my work evolved from redesigning the website and creating a scalable design system to solving a much larger challenge: how to produce consistent, high-quality visual content at scale.

IndustryEcommerce · Fine jewellery
Engagement7 years, ongoing
ToolsFigma · Claude · Magnific
01 · Challenge

Thousands of ring combinations. No photographs to show them.

Auronia is a German maker of highly customizable wedding and engagement rings. Metal, width, stones, engraving: every product exists in many variations, and you can't photograph every combination.

At the time, the brand relied heavily on product renders and stock photography. There was no consistent visual system, no dedicated photography library, and no efficient way to showcase new ring variations across marketing channels. Below is an early snapshot of the brand at that stage.

02 · Role

The website first, then the system around it

I was responsible for UX/UI design, design systems, branding, and later introducing AI-assisted content workflows. Each stage grew out of the last: the website needed a system, the system needed a brand, and the brand needed content we could keep producing.

03 · Foundation

Building a foundation

Previously, Photoshop was the only design tool in use.

I introduced Figma workflows and built a shared design system, establishing a consistent visual language for the website, marketing materials, and future campaigns. This included refreshed brand guidelines covering typography, color, layouts, and photography direction.

Design systems only work when people can actually use them. The goal was a shared scale, clear naming, and a core and semantic structure that stays consistent across the site. We named tokens by what they are rather than where they're used, paired with a shared scale, making the system easier to learn, easier to reuse across the website, and quicker to apply in both Figma and code.

It also made typography rules explicit: every text- token uses Gotham, and every text-heading- token uses Cormorant, so engineers can move fast without guessing. Headings stay simple and scale-only, while body text supports lightweight emphasis through variants.

We followed the same approach for color, rebuilding it into two layers: core and semantic. Core tokens are a simple palette on a shared scale (for example Petrol-50 to Petrol-900) and they only hold raw values. Semantic tokens are what we actually use in designs and code, describing intent across the website (backgrounds, surfaces, borders, text, and states) while always mapping back to core.

The foundation was set. Content production was still the problem.

04 · Brand

Shaping the brand

Regular team meetings aligned the vision and clarified how to apply guidelines across web and social media. Color was one major lever: we replaced a heavy brown palette with softer, welcoming hues evoking love and togetherness. Minimalist typefaces complemented the elegant jewelry aesthetic.

Biggest constraint: no product photography, only renderings and stock imagery to work with.

For a while, we still relied on stock photography, but chosen with intention. I introduced illustration as a bridge to make things feel more personal. Eventually, things shifted: we pulled off our first real photo sessions, and suddenly the brand had a face.

05 · Retail

And just as momentum built, Berlin happened

A brand new store opened, and with it came a whole new challenge: translating everything we'd built (the colors, the mood, the feeling) into a physical space that had to speak the same language. We carried the same token-driven visual language (color, materials, lighting) into the store itself, so walking in felt like stepping inside the website rather than into a separate brand experience.

06 · Voice

From rational to emotional

Before scaling content, we had to decide what it should say. The brand felt very rational and product-focused: it explained how things worked (quality, diamonds, the configurator) but said little about emotion, people and meaning. The tone was informative, technical and reassuring.

Wedding and engagement rings are deeply emotional purchases. The promise was mainly quality and customization, but what the brand is really about is this:

Helping people turn their love story into something they can wear forever.

07 · System

Creating a modular storytelling system

As content volume increased, organization became just as important as creation. I built reusable content structures inside Figma, allowing proposal moments, packaging photography, product renders, educational content, and marketing assets to work together as one connected system. The system transformed content creation from a collection of individual assets into a scalable storytelling framework.

08 · AI

Introducing AI into the creative process

Instead of treating AI as a tool for generating isolated images, I approached it as a scalable content production system. I developed workflows that combined product renders, AI-generated models, and structured prompting to create realistic proposal moments, lifestyle imagery, and marketing assets.

Every prompt followed a short visual identity written for AI: soft natural light and warm neutral tones, an intimate mood with the focus on hands and closeness, premium but never flashy. It's what makes hundreds of generated images feel like one brand.

Behind every finished image is a set of screens like these: one canvas that joins the render with model references, variants generated from the same prompt structure, and frame-by-frame fixes before upscaling.

A key challenge was maintaining product accuracy. Rings needed to be placed naturally on fingers, preserve correct proportions, and match the original designs while interacting realistically with lighting and reflections.

Exploring AI-generated creations allowed us to test visual directions, generate campaign concepts, and showcase ring variations far faster than traditional production methods would have.

This made it easy to present different ring collections consistently, test creative directions quickly, adapt campaigns for different audiences, and maintain visual coherence across channels.

09 · Accuracy

What fails along the way

Many generations don't make it. A ring has to sit on the finger, keep its proportions and match the render exactly, and many images that look fine at first glance break one of these rules. Every frame is checked against the original render before it's used.

  • The ring floats above the fingerinstead of sitting on it
  • The stone changes shapean oval turns round
  • Wrong proportionsthe band is too thick or merges with the finger
  • Handsextra or fused fingers
  • Metal colordrifts between variants

11 · Regulation

Since August 2026, no longer carefree

Article 50 of the EU AI Act now applies: realistic AI images of people or products that could pass as real must be visibly labelled when first shown. So the workflow gained one more step, labelling.

Who is responsible

The brand and the agency. Metadata alone is not enough, and a platform label doesn't shift responsibility.

Low risk

Background, light and layout.

High risk

Changing how the ring looks, and realistic synthetic models.

Sources: AI Act, Art. 50 · European Commission Code of Practice on marking and labelling AI content, 10 June 2026 · label examples are illustrative

12 · Result

Speed and quality, without choosing

By combining branding, design systems, and AI-assisted workflows, Auronia gained a scalable way to create and organize visual content around thousands of customizable products. What used to need a shoot per variation is now produced systematically, down to whole seasonal campaigns.

Most importantly, the project showed how AI can be integrated into a real design process: not as a shortcut, but as a tool for experimentation, storytelling, and scalable content production.

The same system now carries the brand into a new market: the updated US website.
